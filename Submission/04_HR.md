# HR Resource Allocation Plan: Smart Building Automation for Chaac University

## 1. Overview and approach

This report establishes the human resource allocation plan for the Smart Building Automation project at Chaac University by matching the 29 candidates in the Resume Roster to the 40 WBS activities defined in the Task Master. It establishes fixed project start and end dates for every task, enforces a strict workload cap of 40 hours per week per person to eliminate double-booking, structures pre-project training programs to bridge skill gaps prior to Day 1, and identifies single-point-of-failure resource risks along with required mitigations.

### Project baseline

* 40 activities labeled A to AN.
* Project completion: Finishes on Day 390 (Week 78) inside a 392-business-day window, providing 2 days of schedule float/margin.
* 16 critical-path activities: Activities A, B, C, D, I, N, R, U, V, Z, AA, AD, AJ, AK, AM, and AN (all zero float).
* Dependencies: Finish-to-start (FS) for all activities except V and W, which depend on P and O via FS, and depend on T and U via start-to-start with a 35-day lag (SS +35) and finish-to-finish with a 10-day lag (FF +10).

### How the work was done

NotebookLM matched the candidate résumés to the task requirements using the Task Master, Resume Roster, and Allocation Rules documents. The resulting allocations, schedule dates, workloads, and risk factors were cross-checked directly against the Task Master to ensure 100% compliance.

### Allocation rules applied

* Capacity: Standard work hours are 8 hours per day and 40 hours per week (5 business days per week). Project week number equals the project day number divided by 5, rounded up.
* Task load and splits: Each activity requires 1 full-time person (40 hours per week) for its entire duration. Task splits are permitted only in 25% increments (summing to 100%).
* Fixed dates and availability: Task start and end days are fixed as specified in the Task Master. All 29 candidates are available full-time from Day 1 to Day 390.
* Match definitions: Full match requires stated certifications or specific skills directly covering the core skill; Partial match applies when candidate qualifications cover adjacent skills learnable in 12 weeks or less (a primary field name alone does not name a core skill); Gap applies when no candidate covers or can learn the core skill within 12 weeks.
* Training window: Pre-project training for Partial matches is completed prior to Day 1 (Day 0), capped at a maximum of 12 weeks total per person, and does not count against project workload capacity.
* Tie-break hierarchy: Prefers Full matches over Partial matches, higher claimed experience years, lighter concurrent workload, and distribution of critical-path tasks across distinct staff.
* Single-point-of-failure (SPOF) tests: Applied to all resources; triggered if a candidate is the sole match for an assigned task (Test 1), assigned 3 or more critical-path activities (Test 2), or assigned a critical-path task while all alternate matches are at 100% capacity (Test 3).

## 6. Assumptions and limitations

* Hypothetical project execution: The resource allocation plan is a hypothetical modeling exercise that staffs all 40 project activities exclusively using the 29 candidates provided in the Resume Roster.
* Strict evidence grading: Skill matching relies strictly on explicit certifications and named skills in candidate résumés; because most candidates list broad primary fields rather than niche physical automation credentials, 36 of 40 activities are classified as Partial matches.
* Domain skill gaps and external hiring alternatives: Résumés contain limited technical detail on specialized physical systems (BMS architecture, HVAC engineering, IoT field installation, and carbon accounting). The plan assumes all domain capability gaps are fully closeable within 12 weeks of pre-project training with zero external positions opened (OPEN – HIRE). If pre-project training is not acceptable to project leadership, the primary candidates for external hiring would be Task H (BMS Architecture), Task I (HVAC Engineering), Task T (IoT Field Installation), Task U (Smart Controls Installation), Task V (BMS Commissioning), Task W (HVAC Commissioning), Task AF (Energy Modeling), and Task AI (Carbon Accounting).
* Pre-project training scheduling and cost: All training modules are assumed to be available prior to project kickoff (Day 0) and are not costed against the project operational budget.
* Curriculum consolidation: Consolidated training programs for multi-task candidates (Casey W. Morgan, Robin T. Alvarez, Avery D. Brooks, and Riley S. Harper) rely strictly on documented curriculum overlap to keep total training durations at or below the 12-week limit per person.
* Résumé data consistency: Candidate résumés contain varying degrees of detail regarding past project experience and specific tool proficiencies, requiring standardized evaluation across primary fields and certifications.
* Risk mitigations: Risk register mitigations (such as cross-training co-leads and designating floating backups) represent recommended managerial controls and are not hardcoded into task duration estimates or network diagram critical paths.

## 7. How this document was created

We utilized Option 3, which means we used Google NotebookLM. We also used Claude AI to help draft and refine the prompts to make sure we get the outputs we need. We also used it to help identify any inaccuracies that were made by the LM, since it was still possible for it to make hallucinations, even if it had the right data. We believe this is caused by things like the number of files the LM had, as well as the long processing time resulting from the complex work it needed to do. Ultimately NotebookLM was used to generate the final submission, with this section being added and edits being made by us. We utilized Claude to help make the three other documents that were given to NotebookLM. The reasoning behind this action was to isolate the information that the LM needed for this specific work, to help prevent it processing unnecessary information and potential inaccuracies caused by unrelated data. We also used Claude to help compile all the information into a clean document with similar formatting to our previous submissions. It was not allowed to change any data or results, so this document reflects all work done by NotebookLM. NotebookLM struggled with compiling the information into a document, as it faced difficulties with information being cut off, typos, and inconsistencies with what it has already added. The way we solved this difficulty is by making sure that NotebookLM checks its own work against sources and getting the results piece-meal instead of all at once.
